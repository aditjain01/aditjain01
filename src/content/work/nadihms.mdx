## Context

Small clinics in smaller Indian cities mostly run on paper or on software built for large hospitals. Doctors see many patients in a short time, connectivity is unreliable, and any tool that makes the consultation slower will be abandoned. The question this study answers: what would a clinical system look like if the technology disappeared at the point of care?

**Status, plainly:** the architecture is complete and the build has not started.

## My role

Technical and product lead. I own the product thesis, the architecture and the data model.

## Architecture

The thesis is that **technology should be invisible at the point of care.** The doctor talks to the patient. Everything else is the system's job.

On the **clinic device**, an offline-first progressive web app captures voice, transcribes it, and extracts structured encounter fields for the doctor to confirm. **Manual entry is always available as a fallback**, because a doctor must never be blocked by a model that is unsure. Everything is written to a local store first.

A **sync layer** reconciles local writes with the cloud when a connection exists. The cloud holds a **FHIR-aligned data model** across seven domains, with the encounter at the center, so every other record relates to a visit. The design aligns with FHIR R4 and with India's national digital health mission.

## Key decisions and trade-offs

### Offline-first, not online with a cache

**Chose:** local writes first, with sync as a background job.

**Rejected:** a cloud-first app with offline tolerance added later.

**Why:** in the target clinics, an intermittent connection is the normal case. It also makes distribution simpler, since an installable app does not need a hospital IT department. The cost is conflict handling, which has to be designed early.

### Incentives: decision support in return for data

**Chose:** give the doctor useful decision support in exchange for the structured data they would not otherwise record.

**Rejected:** asking doctors to enter data for the benefit of a downstream party.

**Why:** a system that takes without giving gets avoided. The structured record is a by-product of a tool that helps in the moment.

### Voice with a manual fallback

**Chose:** voice to transcript to structured fields, with a plain form always one tap away.

**Rejected:** a voice-only flow, and a form-only flow.

**Why:** voice removes the typing burden, but no extraction pipeline is perfect, and clinical data has to be right. The doctor confirms what is extracted, and the form guarantees a way forward.

### Align with standards from the start

**Chose:** a FHIR R4 aligned schema and alignment with the national digital health mission.

**Rejected:** a private schema with a conversion layer planned for later.

**Why:** retrofitting a standard is more expensive than designing for it, and interoperability is what makes records useful beyond one clinic.

### The encounter as the center of the schema

**Chose:** seven domains, all related through the encounter.

**Rejected:** a patient-centric model with visits as child records.

**Why:** the clinical workflow is organized around visits. An encounter-centered model matches how data is created and how it is reviewed.

## Outcomes

There are no outcomes yet. This is a design study. If it is built, I would measure:

- Time added to a consultation, with the goal of none.
- Share of encounters where the doctor confirms extracted fields without edits.
- Share of encounters completed fully offline.

## What I'd do next, and what I learned

Next: validate the voice pipeline against real recordings in the target languages before building anything else, since that is the riskiest assumption.

What I learned: designing for the constraint, here an unreliable connection and a doctor with no spare attention, produces a simpler product than starting from features.
